## Everyday situations that simulation could analyze

Many everyday situations are queueing systems, for example a bus stop, a bank, a hospital emergency department, a coffee shop and a **supermarket checkout area**.

In the supermarket, customers arrive, wait in a queue at a checkout, get served by the cashier and leave. A simulation could answer questions like:

- How long does a customer wait on average?
- What happens to the queues if a second checkout is opened?
- What happens on Friday afternoon when many people shop after work?

## Why simulation is better than experimenting in the real world

- **Cost:** adding cashiers or self-checkouts in real life costs money. In a model, we only change a number.
- **Time:** a real experiment needs days or weeks. A simulation runs in seconds.
- **Control:** real customer arrivals change every day (weather, holidays, offers). In a simulation, the same conditions can be repeated to compare options fairly.
- **No risk:** a bad real experiment gives customers long queues and they may stop shopping there. A failed simulation affects nobody.
- **Variability:** arrival and service times are random, and the parts of the system affect each other (one slow cashier makes the other queues longer). This is hard to calculate by hand but easy to simulate.

## Aspects of sustainability in the simulation

- **Environmental:** save energy by measuring how long checkouts are idle, and reduce food waste by predicting how many customers will come and how much to stock.
- **Economic:** compare the cost of different numbers of cashiers with the waiting time they give.
- **Social:** keep waiting times short, and make sure there are enough checkouts for people who need more time, such as elderly customers.

These can be measured in the model as outputs, such as average waiting time, idle time of a checkout and the number of customers served.
